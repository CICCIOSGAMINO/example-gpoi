United States
==============
[TOC]

Unlike the centralized, treaty-based frameworks of the EU or China, the U.S. approach to digital technology governance is decentralized, sector-specific, and largely reactive. There is no single federal digital ministry or comprehensive omnibus law comparable to the GDPR. Instead, the American system is a patchwork of federal statutes targeting specific issues (like health data or children's privacy), executive branch policy directives, voluntary industry standards, and a growing number of state-level laws, with California often leading the way. The country's strategy for technologies like AI is driven by a strong emphasis on private-sector innovation, maintaining global technological leadership, and a light-touch regulatory touch that is currently in flux, aiming to preempt a rapidly emerging patchwork of state regulations.

# Key Laws and Regulations

## Foundational Data Privacy Laws (Sectoral Approach)

Unlike the EU’s GDPR, the United States lacks a comprehensive federal data privacy law.Instead, privacy protection is sectoral:

- **Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA – 1996):** Governs the use and disclosure of protected health information by covered entities and their business associates.- **Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA – 1999):** Regulates financial institutions’ collection, use and disclosure of consumers’ personal financial information.- **Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA – 1998):** Requires parental consent for collection of personal information from children under 13.- **Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA – 1970):** Regulates the collection, dissemination and use of consumer credit information.- **Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA – 1986):** Governs law enforcement access to electronic communications and associated data.- **Video Privacy Protection Act (VPPA – 1988):** Restricts disclosure of personally identifiable rental records for video materials.- **Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA – 1991):** Restricts telemarketing calls, auto-dialed calls and text messages.- **CAN-SPAM Act (2003):** Establishes requirements for commercial email messages.- **Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA – 2008):** Prohibits discrimination based on genetic information in health insurance and employment.
## Federal Privacy Reform Efforts (2025–2026)

Several comprehensive federal privacy bills have been proposed but not yet enacted:

- **American Data Privacy and Protection Act (ADPPA):** Would create a comprehensive federal consumer privacy and security framework, generally preempting state laws that are covered by its provisions, while preserving 16 categories of state laws including specific ones in Illinois and California. It would provide enforcement by the FTC, state attorneys general, and in some cases individuals.
- **Online Privacy Act (Re‑introduced March 2026):** Sets a national baseline for how Americans’ personal data can be collected, used, and shared. Gives users the right to access, correct, delete, and transfer data; establishes a Digital Privacy Agency (DPA); and criminalizes doxxing.
- **Government Surveillance Reform Act (GSRA):** Reintroduced with strong bipartisan support, would stop federal agencies from buying Americans’ most personal data from data brokers without a warrant, while reforming Section 702 of FISA. It also extends warrant requirements to location information, web browsing data, search and chatbot records.

## Artificial Intelligence (AI) Governance

### Strategic Executive Actions

- **Executive Order 14179 (January 23, 2025):** "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence," establishing the policy of sustaining and enhancing U.S. global AI dominance and directing the development of an AI Action Plan.
- **America’s AI Action Plan (July 2025):** A policy roadmap anchored in EO 14179, built on three pillars: innovation, infrastructure, and international diplomacy and security. It calls for streamlined permitting for data centers and semiconductor manufacturing facilities, emphasizes export controls on advanced AI chips, and promotes global AI dominance through deregulation and technology export.
- **Executive Order 14365 (December 11, 2025):** "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence," initiating a coordinated federal review of state‑level AI laws and directing agencies to develop policy recommendations, standards and legislative proposals to inform a national approach.
- **National Policy Framework for AI: Legislative Recommendations (March 20, 2026):** A comprehensive legislative blueprint urging Congress to establish a uniform federal standard for AI and preempt a growing patchwork of state AI laws. The framework is built around six policy areas: child protection, energy/electricity costs, intellectual property, free speech concerns, education and workforce readiness, and favors a lighter‑touch federal approach over a new standalone AI regulator.

### Legislative Proposals

- **TRUMP AMERICA AI Act (March 2026):** A 291‑page discussion draft by Senator Marsha Blackburn, proposing annual bias audits for AI used in employment decisions, quarterly workforce displacement reporting, a new federal liability regime for AI developers and deployers, and a legislative resolution of the copyright training data question. The bill protects the "4 Cs" (children, creators, conservatives, and communities) and includes provisions such as a duty of care on AI developers to prevent foreseeable harm to minors and sunsetting Section 230.

### Technical Standards

- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF, January 2023):** A voluntary, cross‑industry framework to improve AI trustworthiness and manage AI‑related risks.
- **NIST Cyber AI Profile (Preliminary Draft, December 2025):** Aligned with NIST’s Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0, it addresses AI‑specific risks across three focus areas: securing AI system components, conducting AI‑enabled cyber defense, and thwarting AI‑enabled cyber attacks.
- **Financial Services AI Risk Management Framework (FS AI RMF, March 2026):** Developed by the Treasury Department’s AIEOG, it includes a questionnaire and a matrix of 230 control objectives specifically for banks and financial services firms.

### Institutional Developments

- **Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI – formerly the U.S. AI Safety Institute, renamed June 2025):** A NIST‑managed center that evaluates U.S. and adversary AI systems and supports best practices development.
- **NIST AI Economic Security Centers (December 2025):** A $20 million award established two new AI centers: the AI Center for U.S. Manufacturing Productivity and the AI Center to Secure U.S. Critical Infrastructure from Cyberthreats, in partnership with MITRE.

## Cybersecurity Policy

### Executive Orders

- **Executive Order 14306 (June 6, 2025):** "Sustaining Select Efforts to Strengthen the Nation's Cybersecurity," amending Biden‑era EO 14144 and Obama‑era EO 13694. It focuses on critical protections against foreign cyber threats, enhances secure technology practices, limits cyber sanctions to foreign malicious actors, and refocuses AI cybersecurity efforts towards identifying and managing vulnerabilities rather than censorship.
- **Biden‑era Executive Order 14144 (January 16, 2025):** Issued in the waning days of the Biden administration, it sought to mandate private‑sector attestation of secure software development practices, improve federal agency cybersecurity, and accelerate adoption of post‑quantum encryption and AI for cybersecurity functions, but many of its efforts were not taken up by the Trump Administration.

### Critical Infrastructure Protection

- **Cyber Deterrence and Response Act of 2025 (Introduced December 2025):** Deters foreign nation‑states from launching cyberattacks against critical American infrastructure by creating a unified federal process to identify, attribute, and sanction state‑sponsored cyber actors, and imposes robust sanctions including asset blocking, financial restrictions, and visa bans.

## Digital Economy and Semiconductors

- **CHIPS and Science Act (August 2022):** Signed by President Joe Biden, making up to $52 billion available to incentivize semiconductor makers to establish new domestic manufacturing operations, train 110,000 workers, and protect supply chains from disruption by foreign rivals. The Trump administration plans to continue the program while removing "all extraneous policy requirements" for CHIPS‑funded projects. OpenAI has requested expansion of the CHIPS Act tax credit to cover AI servers, data centers, and grid hardware.
- **Semiconductor Export Controls (Ongoing):** Bipartisan efforts in Congress to prevent easing of curbs on AI chip sales to China, including proposed legislation to block the Trump administration from relaxing rules for Nvidia and AMD chips for up to 2.5 years.

## State‑Level Privacy and AI Laws

As of early 2026, 19 states have enacted comprehensive consumer privacy laws, with more legislation pending. Each state law contains different obligations, exemptions, scope provisions and enforcement mechanisms, creating a complex compliance patchwork for businesses.

### California (Leading Role)

California continues to shape U.S. privacy regulation through the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), amended by the California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA). Effective January 1, 2026, new CPPA regulations introduce:

- Mandatory cybersecurity audits for certain business categories
- Risk assessments for automated decision‑making technology (ADMT)
- Expanded rights for consumers related to algorithmic processes
- New monetary thresholds and increased civil penalties for violations

Recent California legislation also includes neural data within the "sensitive personal information" category (SB 1223 and AB 1008).

### Other Key State Laws

- **Indiana, Kentucky, Rhode Island:** New comprehensive consumer privacy statutes effective January 1, 2026.
- **Delaware and Oregon:** Statutory requirements mandating recognition of universal opt‑out preference signals effective January 1, 2026.
- **Florida AI Bill of Rights and Data Centers Bill (2026)**
- **Texas Senate Bill 1188 (AI regulation)**
- **New York AI Companion Models Act and Algorithmic Pricing Disclosure Act (2026)**

### Federal Preemption Push

The Trump administration’s National Policy Framework for AI (March 2026) calls for Congress to preempt state AI laws deemed unduly burdensome or inconsistent with federal policy, while preserving state authority over generally applicable consumer protection, fraud, and child safety laws, as well as state control over procurement and internal governmental use of AI.

# Institutions

- **Federal Trade Commission (FTC):** Primary federal consumer protection agency with authority over privacy and data security enforcement. Proposed federal privacy laws designate the FTC as the primary enforcement agency.

- **Department of Commerce (DoC):** Oversees the CHIPS program, semiconductor manufacturing incentives, and through NIST develops voluntary AI risk management frameworks, standards, and technical guidance. DoC’s Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) evaluates AI systems and supports best practices.

- **National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST):** Develops voluntary cybersecurity and AI frameworks including the Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0, AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), and the Cyber AI Profile. NIST’s AI Economic Security Centers accelerate AI deployment in manufacturing and critical infrastructure cybersecurity.

- **Department of Homeland Security (DHS):** Through the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), responsible for protecting critical infrastructure from cyber threats. The AI Action Plan tasks DHS with establishing an AI Information Sharing and Analysis Center and creating industry guidance on AI‑specific vulnerabilities.

- **Office of the National Cyber Director (ONCD):** Coordinates federal cybersecurity policy and strategy across agencies, working with DHS and DoC on AI‑security threat information sharing and vulnerability management.

- **Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA):** Leads national efforts to protect critical infrastructure from physical and cyber threats, operates threat‑hunt operations, and coordinates vulnerability information sharing.

- **National Cyber Director (within the Executive Office of the President):** Formally identifies foreign persons responsible for significant cyberattacks under the Cyber Deterrence and Response Act, and coordinates the attribution framework.

- **California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA):** California’s independent privacy regulator with authority to adopt regulations under the CCPA and CPRA, enforce privacy laws, and issue administrative fines.

- **Department of Justice (DOJ):** Through its AI Litigation Task Force (established under EO 14365), actively challenges state AI laws that burden innovation and fragment the national regulatory landscape.

- **Department of the Treasury – Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group (AIEOG):** A public‑private partnership developing AI risk management resources for the financial services sector, including the FS AI RMF and a shared AI Lexicon.

# Glossary

- **AI RMF:** NIST AI Risk Management Framework – a voluntary, cross‑industry framework published January 2023 to improve AI trustworthiness and manage AI‑related risks.

- **AI Action Plan:** America’s AI Action Plan (July 2025) – the Trump administration’s policy roadmap anchored in EO 14179, aiming for "global AI dominance" through deregulation, infrastructure build‑out, and technology export.

- **CAISI:** Center for AI Standards and Innovation – formerly the U.S. AI Safety Institute (renamed June 2025), a NIST‑managed center that evaluates U.S. and adversary AI systems.

- **CCPA:** California Consumer Privacy Act (2018) – California’s comprehensive consumer privacy law, amended by the CPRA.

- **CHIPS Act:** CHIPS and Science Act (August 2022) – provides up to $52 billion to incentivize domestic semiconductor manufacturing, worker training, and supply chain resilience.

- **COPPA:** Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act (1998) – requires parental consent for collection of personal information from children under 13.

- **CPPA:** California Privacy Protection Agency – California’s independent privacy regulator, responsible for CCPA/CPRA enforcement and rule‑making.

- **CSF 2.0:** NIST Cybersecurity Framework version 2.0 – a voluntary framework for managing cybersecurity risk, used as the structural basis for the Cyber AI Profile.

- **Cyber AI Profile:** NIST IR 8596 (preliminary draft December 2025) – aligns CSF 2.0 outcomes to AI‑specific risks across secure, defend, and thwart focus areas.

- **Data Broker Loophole:** A practice whereby federal agencies purchase Americans’ personal data from data brokers without a warrant, which the GSRA aims to close.

- **FCRA:** Fair Credit Reporting Act (1970) – regulates the collection, dissemination and use of consumer credit information.

- **FISA Section 702:** Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act provision authorizing warrantless surveillance of non‑U.S. persons outside the United States, subject to ongoing reform efforts including warrant requirements for searches of Americans’ communications.

- **FS AI RMF:** Financial Services AI Risk Management Framework (March 2026) – Treasury‑developed framework with 230 control objectives specifically for financial institutions, operationalizing the NIST AI RMF for the banking sector.

- **GLBA:** Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (1999) – regulates financial institutions’ collection, use and disclosure of consumers’ personal financial information.

- **HIPAA:** Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (1996) – governs the use and disclosure of protected health information.

- **NDO Fairness Act:** Proposed bipartisan legislation protecting Americans from law enforcement accessing individuals’ private data without disclosure, based on Fourth Amendment protections against unreasonable searches.

- **Neural Data:** Data generated by measuring the activity of a consumer’s central or peripheral nervous system. Recently added to California’s "sensitive personal information" category via SB 1223 and AB 1008 (October 2025).

- **Online Privacy Act (2026):** Re‑introduced March 2026 by Rep. Zoe Lofgren, proposes a national baseline for data privacy including establishment of a Digital Privacy Agency (DPA) and private right of action.

- **Patchwork (State Privacy/AI Laws):** The emerging collection of different, often conflicting state‑level privacy and AI regulations across 19+ states, which the federal government seeks to preempt with a uniform national framework.

- **Preemption (State Law Preemption):** A key policy objective of the Trump administration’s National AI Framework, calling for Congress to override conflicting state AI laws while preserving state authority over generally applicable consumer protection and local procurement.

- **Sectoral Approach (U.S. Privacy Law):** The U.S. regulatory model of addressing privacy through domain‑specific federal statutes (HIPAA, GLBA, COPPA, FCRA, etc.) rather than a single omnibus law like the GDPR.

- **TRUMP AMERICA AI Act:** Senator Marsha Blackburn’s 291‑page discussion draft (March 2026) proposing annual bias audits, workforce displacement reporting, a new liability regime, and copyright resolution – a parallel legislative vehicle to the White House framework.
